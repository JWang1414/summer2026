# From one to many
Lets say we are now interested in seeing how the pigeons behaves returning to the pigeonholes a number of times. For just a single trip, we have the two possibilities: $p_{1}(L)$ and $p_{1}(R)$. However with two trips the sample space expands into $\Sigma_{2}=\{ L L, LR, RL, RR \}$. Our task is to define a new probability function $p_{2}$ for this sample space.

It is tempting to claim $p_{2}(LL)=p_{1}(L)p_{1}(L)$. However, this is not always true. Generally speaking, the probability is:
$$
	p_{2}(ba) = \tilde{p}(b|a)p_{1}(a)
$$
Where $\tilde{p}(b|a)$ is the *conditional probability*, the chance of $b$ occurring given that $a$ has already occurred.

Iff the conditional probability $\tilde{p}(b|a)=q(b)$ for some function $q$, the probability of measuring $b$ on the second trial doesn't depend on the outcome of the first trial. The two trials are said to be independent.

The probability of an outcome from $n$ repeated trials is:
$$
	p_{n}(a_{n}\dots a_{1}) = \tilde{p}(a_{n}|a_{n-1}\dots a_{1}) \dots \tilde{p}(a_{2}|a_{1}) p_{1}(a_{1})
$$
In the case of *independent identically distributed* trials, this simplifies to:
$$
	p_{n}(a_{n}\dots a_{1}) = p_{1}(a_{n}) p_{1}(a_{n-1}) \dots p_{1}(a_{2})p_{1}(a_{1})
$$

Now, imagine we have $n$ iid pigeons. The pigeons have identical preferences, and their choices are independent of other pigeons. For this collection of pigeons, the average surprise is $n$ times the average surprise of a single pigeon:
$$
	\left< s_{n} \right> =n\left< s_{1} \right>
$$
# Random variable to random process
A *random process* rp is a set of random variables rv $\{ X_{k} \}$. In general, each random variable in this set can have its own probability function $p_{k}(i)$. Physically, imagine an rp as a set of rv generated by repeated trials.

A special case of rp is the iid process. All its rv have identical probability functions $p_{k}(i)=p(i)$. And all its rv are independent, $\left< X_{i}X_{j} \right> = \left< X_{i} \right>\left< X_{j} \right>$ for any pair of rv in the process.

Consider the *random walk*. We have a sample space $s \in \{ 1, -1 \}$ with the probability function $p(1)=p(-1)=0.5$. Define the rv $X=x_{0}s$ where $x_{0}$ is some quantity with dimensions of length. This random variable has:
$$
	\left< X \right> =0 \qquad \text{var}(X) = \left< X^{2} \right> =x_{0}^{2}
$$
This is basically describing the process of flipping a coin, and depending on the outcome, take a step to the left or right.

Now consider the random process $\{ X_{k} \}$, where $k\in \{ 1, 2, \dots, N \}$. So we have $N$ repetitions of these iid trials. The total displacement from this walk is $X_\text{tot}=\sum_{k}X_{k}$. The average total displacement must be zero:
$$
	\left< X_\text{tot} \right>  = \left< \sum_{k} X_{k} \right> = \sum_{k} \left< X_{k} \right> =0
$$
However, the variance is positive:
$$
	\text{var}(X_\text{tot}) = \left< X^{2}_\text{tot} \right> = \sum_{k} \left< X^{2}_{k} \right> + \sum_{i} \sum_{j} \left< X_{i}X_{j} \right> = \sum_{k} \left< X^{2}_{k} \right> + \sum_{i} \sum_{j} \left< X_{i} \right> \left< X_{j} \right>
$$
Just like above, this last summation will collapse to zero, and the variance is:
$$
	\text{var}(X_\text{tot}) = Nx_{0}^{2} \implies \sigma(X_\text{tot}) = x_{0}\sqrt{ N }
$$
So, despite the average value of the total displacement staying at zero, the variance will progressively increase as you take more and more steps.
# Micro-Macro Rule
Consider $N$ particles in a box with left half $L$ and right half $R$. Lets try to assign a probability to the untracked dof, focusing on the position of the particles.

We have no information on the particles other than that they are at equilibrium inside the box. So, it is more natural to assign the most unbiased probability function $p(L)=p(R)=0.5$. The average surprise for this probability function is $\left< s \right> = \log 2$. If the $N$ particles inside are iid, then the average surprise for the entire ensemble is $\left< s \right> = N\log 2$.

Imagine we know all particles are in the left half of the box. Then, $p(L)=1$ and $p(R)=0$. The average surprise becomes $\left< s \right> =0$. And the change in the average surprise due to this new information is $\Delta \left< s \right> = -N\log 2$.

Recall that, when we beat the Carnot limit, this is exactly what we did. By completing a Carnot cycle without the entropy cost of isothermal compression, we compute $\Delta S_\text{cyc}=-Nk_{B}\log 2$, lower than the $\Delta S_\text{cyc}=0$ for the Carnot cycle.

The similarity between these two quantities motivates the definition of a physical postulate.

Micro-Macro Rule:
> The entropy of a cycle is equal to the average surprise of the probability function assigned to its untracked dof.
$$
	S=k_{B}\left< s \right>
$$
## Algorithm for equilibrium thermal physics
The algorithm for calculating the equilibrium properties of any system is as follows:
1. Identify the untracked dof of the system, and encode the available knowledge about the system in the form of constraints.
2. Find the most unbiased probability function 𝑝on the sample space of the untracked dof, consistent with the constraints. Calculate the average surprise $\left< s \right>$ for this probability function.
3. Use $S=k_{B}\left< s \right>$, along with the definition of macro thermodynamic quantities in terms of $S$, to calculate whatever you need.
